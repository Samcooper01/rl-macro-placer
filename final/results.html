rl-macro-placer, V6: scaling study (and why the first version was wrong)

Does the GNN (Graph Neural Network) advantage emerge as problems get larger? A first, under-powered version of this study said yes. A properly-powered replication says no. This page reports both, honestly.

The first attempt (and its mistake)

An initial study at three scales (4, 8, 14 macros) with a 25-problem test set suggested a clean trend: the GNN improvement rose 1.3% to 2.0% to 2.4% while the baseline stayed flat. It looked like the GNN advantage emerging with scale. But three points with a small test set and a single training seed is weak evidence, so the study was rerun with more points and more samples.

The replication

Six scales (4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14 macros), a 40-problem held-out test set at each, same 100k-step training budget per run. Twelve training runs total.

The earlier finding did not replicate. With proper sampling there is no GNN advantage: the flat baseline matches or beats the GNN at every scale, and the tidy monotonic rise from the three-point study is gone (it was noise from a small test set and a single seed). Both architectures instead decline as scale grows, reaching no-better-than-random by 14 macros.

Why both decline: a confound this study cannot escape

The decline of both lines to roughly random at 14 macros is the key signal, and it is a confound, not a conclusion about architecture. Every scale got the same 100k training budget, but larger problems have longer episodes and a much larger action space, so they need far more training to learn anything. At 14 macros the budget is simply too small to train either policy, so both collapse toward random. This means the study, as designed with a fixed budget, cannot properly test the scale hypothesis: the larger problems are undertrained, which masks any real effect.

Honest bottom line

  • At an accessible, fixed training budget there is no GNN advantage over the flat baseline at any tested scale.
  • The first study's positive trend was a false positive from under-sampling; the replication corrected it. This is the value of asking for more data points.
  • Whether the GNN advantage would emerge at real scale remains genuinely open, because a fair test requires a training budget that grows with problem size, which requires moving the environment off the CPU (Central Processing Unit): a GPU (Graphics Processing Unit) tensor rewrite of the force-directed placement and metrics, plus parallel compute. That is the honest next step, and this study is the evidence that a laptop-scale, fixed-budget experiment cannot settle the question.
